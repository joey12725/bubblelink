How the BMS cuts power

The whole stick is one loop: out of the battery's positive rail, through the loads, and back into its negative rail. There are two switches in that loop. The key breaks the + side by hand. The BMS breaks the − side automatically. Either one opens the loop, and no current flows anywhere. Pick a state:

Two switches, one loop

  • The key sits on the + side and you work it by hand.
  • The BMS sits on the − side and works automatically: a cell too low, a short, too much current, or overcharging.
  • Current needs the whole circle, so opening either one stops it everywhere.

Why the BMS is on the − side

Breaking the loop anywhere stops the current, and nearly all small lithium BMS boards break it on the negative side. The switches they use (N-channel MOSFETs) are cheaper and more efficient there. So the + side never goes through the BMS: the positive rail goes straight to the key, and the charger's + goes straight to the rail.

What this means while you wire

  • The + side is always live, even when the BMS has shut off: the positive rail, KEY pin A, and the charger lead.
  • Every − goes to P−. A return wired to the negative rail skips the BMS. Try the last button.
  • Keep + wires off the negative rail and the cell cans. An 18650's can is its negative terminal, so a short there has no BMS protection.

P− means "pack minus"

It's the negative that everything outside the battery sees: the − splice for the loads, and the charger. Your BMS label says 同口 ("common port"): charging and the loads share P−, so the same switch protects both.
